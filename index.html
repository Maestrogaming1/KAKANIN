BIBINGKA

Bibingka is a baked rice cake traditionally cooked in clay pots lined with banana leaves.

SUMAN

Sticky rice steamed in banana leaves, often served with sugar or latik.

ESPASOL

Cylinder-shaped treat made from rice flour and coconut milk, dusted with toasted rice powder.

SAPIN-SAPIN

A multi-colored, layered glutinous rice and coconut dessert.

BIKO

Biko is a sweet, sticky rice cake made from malagkit (glutinous rice) and coconut milk. It is topped with a thick, dark caramel layer called latik or a gooey coconut syrup called arnibal.

Cooking Procedures

How to make Bibingka

Step 1: Combine rice flour, sugar, and baking powder in a bowl.

Step 2: Add coconut milk, melted butter, and eggs. Mix until smooth.

Step 3: Pour into banana-leaf lined pans and bake until golden.

Step 4: Bake (traditionally with coals on top and bottom) and top with salted egg and cheese halfway through

How to make Suman

Step 1: Soak glutinous rice in water for 1-2 hours.

Step 2: Mix with coconut milk and salt; cook until liquid is absorbed.

Step 3: Wrap in banana leaves and steam for 45 minutes.

Step 4: Steam the wrapped bundle for 45 to 60 minutes until fully sticky and tender.

How to make Espasol

Step 1: Toast rice flour in a dry pan over medium heat until light brown and fragrant. Set aside some for dusting.

Step 2: Boil coconut milk and sugar in a large pan until it thickens into a syrup.

Step 3: Stir in the toasted rice flour and shredded coconut. Mix constantly until the dough becomes very thick and heavy.

Step 4: Roll the mixture into cylinders and coat generously with the reserved toasted flour.

How to make Sapin-Sapin

Step 1: Mix glutinous rice flour, coconut milk, and condensed milk until smooth. Divide the mixture into three bowls.

Step 2: Color one bowl purple (Ube), one yellow (Jackfruit/Langka), and leave one white (Coconut).

Step 3: Steam the purple layer in a greased pan for 15 minutes. Pour the white layer on top and steam for another 15 minutes.

Step 4: Add the final yellow layer, steam until set, and top with toasted latik (coconut curds).

How to make Biko

Step 1: Wash glutinous rice and cook it with less water than usual (so it stays slightly firm).

Step 2: In a separate pan, boil coconut milk and brown sugar over medium heat until the mixture thickens into a syrup.

Step 3: Combine the cooked rice into the syrup and stir constantly until the rice is fully coated and very sticky.

Step 4: Spread onto a tray and top with latik (fried coconut milk solids) or a separate caramel glaze.

History of the Kakanin





The history of Kakanin is a story of resilience, adaptation, and the profound connection between the Filipino people and their land. The term itself, derived from kanin (prepared rice) and the suffix -in, literally means "to eat rice," but its cultural weight far exceeds its simple definition. For centuries, these glutinous rice cakes have served as the centerpiece of Philippine celebrations, a testament to the country’s agricultural heritage and its spiritual evolution. Before the arrival of Spanish colonizers in the 16th century, kakanin was deeply rooted in indigenous spirituality. In pre-colonial society, rice was viewed as a sacred gift from the deities. Our ancestors did not just consume rice for survival; they transformed it into elaborate offerings called alay to appease the anitos (ancestral spirits) and ensure a bountiful harvest. The foundational technique of galapong—where glutinous rice is soaked and then stone-ground into a thick, smooth paste—dates back to this era. This traditional method, which requires patience and physical labor, remains the essential soul of authentic kakanin, providing the distinct chewy texture that modern flour-based substitutes cannot replicate. With the onset of the Spanish colonial period, kakanin underwent a fascinating transformation. Rather than being suppressed, these indigenous treats were integrated into the Catholic liturgical calendar. The most iconic example is the Simbang Gabi, or the series of dawn masses leading to Christmas. As farmers emerged from early morning prayers to head to the fields, they were greeted by the aroma of bibingka and puto bumbong cooking over charcoal. These rice cakes provided the warmth and sustained energy needed for a day of hard labor. Over time, these snacks transitioned from being "food for the masses" to becoming indispensable symbols of the Filipino Christmas spirit, blending ancient rice-growing traditions with Christian devotion. The geography of the Philippines, an archipelago of over 7,000 islands, naturally led to a rich regional diversity of kakanin. Each province utilized its local bounty to create unique variations. In the north, the Ilocanos developed Tupig, a grilled rice cake wrapped in banana leaves that allowed for easy transport. In the central plains of Luzon, Kapampangans perfected Tibok-tibok, a velvety white pudding made from fresh carabao milk. Further south in the Visayas and Mindanao, the abundance of coconut and root crops led to delicacies like Binagol and Kalamay. This regionalism turned kakanin into a culinary map of the Philippines, where every bite tells a story of the specific soil and climate of its origin. The "holy trinity"of kakanin ingredients—rice, coconut, and sugar—defines its identity. The coconut tree, the "tree of life," provides the gata (milk) for richness and the latik (toasted curds) for a salty-sweet contrast. While modern kakanin often uses refined sugar, the traditional use of panutsa (palm sugar) or muscovado lends an earthy, molasses-like depth to the flavor. In the modern era, kakanin continues to bridge the gap between the past and the present. While it has moved from bamboo steamers to high-end restaurant menus, its essence remains unchanged. It is more than just a merienda (snack); it is a vessel for pakikisama (fellowship) and a delicious reminder of a history that began in the rice paddies and continues to thrive at every Filipino gathering.
